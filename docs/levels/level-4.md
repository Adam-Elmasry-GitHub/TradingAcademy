## Lessons

### 4.1 Company fundamentals

A stock's long-term value comes from the company's future cash flows. Key numbers are revenue (sales), earnings (profit), earnings per share (EPS), margins, debt and free cash flow.

The P/E ratio (price divided by earnings per share) shows how much investors pay for each unit of profit. A high P/E means the market expects growth; a low one can mean value or trouble. Compare P/E within the same industry.

For traders, earnings reports are volatility events. A stock can gap 10-20% overnight on results. The surprise versus expectations matters more than whether the numbers are 'good'.

Use fundamentals to choose what to trade (strong companies for longs, weak ones for shorts) and when to avoid holding (just before earnings, unless that is your plan).

**Key takeaways**

- Value comes from future cash flows.
- Markets react to surprises versus expectations.
- Know the earnings dates of everything you hold.

**Practice:** Find the next earnings date and the analyst EPS estimate for one stock you follow.

**Read more:** Benjamin Graham, 'The Intelligent Investor'; Aswath Damodaran's free valuation course (pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar)

### 4.2 Macro: interest rates, inflation and central banks

Central banks (Norges Bank, the ECB, the US Federal Reserve) set short-term interest rates to control inflation. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, which tends to slow the economy and pressure stock valuations, especially growth stocks.

Currencies react to interest rate differences. If Norges Bank raises rates more than expected, NOK often strengthens. The US dollar is affected by Fed decisions and is the world's main reserve currency.

Inflation reports (CPI), employment data (for example US non-farm payrolls) and GDP growth are key macro releases. Markets move on the difference between the actual number and the forecast.

Norway is closely linked to oil and gas. Oil prices affect Equinor, Aker BP, the Oslo Børs index and the krone. This is called intermarket analysis: how markets influence each other.

**Key takeaways**

- Rates up usually means pressure on stocks and support for the currency.
- Markets move on surprises versus forecasts.
- Oil matters a lot for Norwegian markets and NOK.

**Practice:** Compare a chart of Brent oil with EQNR and USD/NOK over the last year. Describe the relationship.

**Read more:** Norges Bank: monetary policy reports (norges-bank.no); Federal Reserve Education (federalreserveeducation.org); Ray Dalio, 'How the Economic Machine Works' (free video)

### 4.3 News, the economic calendar and sentiment

An economic calendar lists scheduled releases with forecasts and previous values. Before trading any day, check it. Spreads widen and price can jump violently around big releases.

Beginners should not trade the release itself. Either be flat before major news or size down so that a gap through your stop is survivable.

Sentiment is the mood of market participants. Measures include the VIX ('fear index'), put/call ratios and surveys like the AAII sentiment survey. Extreme fear or greed is often a contrarian signal.

'Buy the rumour, sell the news': prices often move before an expected event and reverse after it. Ask what is already priced in.

**Key takeaways**

- Check the economic calendar every trading day.
- Avoid or size down around major news.
- Extreme sentiment often marks turning points.

**Practice:** Pick one high-impact release this month. Write your expectation, then compare it with the actual move.

**Read more:** Investing.com or ForexFactory economic calendar; CBOE: 'VIX Index' education pages (cboe.com)

### 4.4 Combining fundamentals and technicals

Fundamentals answer 'what' and 'why'; technicals answer 'when' and 'where'. A powerful approach is to choose instruments with a fundamental reason to move, then use the chart to time entries and set stops.

Example: a company beats earnings and raises guidance (fundamental catalyst). Price gaps up and then consolidates in a flag (technical structure). You buy the breakout with a stop below the flag.

Relative strength: compare a stock to its index. Stocks that hold up when the market falls often lead when it rises. This is the basis of many professional momentum strategies.

Keep a top-down view: global macro, then sector, then individual instrument. Trade in the direction where all three agree.

**Key takeaways**

- Fundamentals for what to trade, technicals for when.
- Relative strength reveals leaders.
- Best trades have macro, sector and chart aligned.

**Practice:** Build a watchlist of 10 stocks that are stronger than their index over the last 3 months.

**Read more:** William O'Neil, 'How to Make Money in Stocks' (CAN SLIM); Mark Minervini, 'Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard'
