## Lessons

### 5.1 Cognitive biases

Loss aversion: losses hurt roughly twice as much as equal gains feel good (Kahneman and Tversky). This makes traders hold losers too long, hoping to get back to break-even, and sell winners too early.

Confirmation bias: we look for information that supports our position and ignore what contradicts it. Fix: before entering, write what would prove you wrong.

Recency bias: the last few trades feel more important than the last hundred. Three losses in a row do not mean your strategy stopped working; a few hundred trades might.

Overconfidence: after a winning streak, traders increase size and break rules. Some of the biggest losses come right after the best weeks.

**Key takeaways**

- Loss aversion makes us hold losers and cut winners.
- Define what proves you wrong before entering.
- Judge a strategy on a large sample, not the last few trades.

**Practice:** Review your journal: find one trade affected by each bias above.

**Read more:** Daniel Kahneman, 'Thinking, Fast and Slow'; Mark Douglas, 'Trading in the Zone'

### 5.2 Fear, greed, FOMO and revenge trading

Fear makes you skip valid setups or exit too early. Greed makes you oversize or move your target further away. FOMO (fear of missing out) makes you chase a move that already happened, entering late with a bad stop.

Revenge trading is trying to win back a loss immediately, usually with bigger size and a worse setup. It is the fastest route to a blown account.

Practical defences: a maximum number of trades per day, a daily loss limit after which you stop, a mandatory break after two losses in a row, and fixed position sizing calculated before entry.

Mark Douglas taught thinking in probabilities: any single trade's result is random; only the series matters. When you truly accept that, a loss stops feeling personal.

**Key takeaways**

- Name the emotion before you act on it.
- Hard rules (daily loss limit, max trades) beat willpower.
- Each trade is one of the next hundred.

**Practice:** Add a rule to your plan: 'After 2 losses in a row I stop for the day.' Follow it in the simulator.

**Read more:** Brett Steenbarger, 'The Daily Trading Coach'; Jack Schwager, 'Market Wizards' (interviews on discipline)

### 5.3 The trading plan

A trading plan is a written document that answers: what markets and timeframes I trade; my setups with exact entry, stop and exit rules; my risk per trade, per day and per week; when I trade and when I do not; and how I review.

If a rule cannot be checked as 'followed' or 'not followed', it is not a rule. 'Buy strong stocks' is vague; 'Buy when price closes above the 20-day high while above the 50 SMA' is a rule.

Your plan turns trading from emotional decisions into executing a process. Your goal each day becomes 'follow the plan', not 'make money'.

Review and update the plan monthly, based on journal data, not on feelings after one bad day.

**Key takeaways**

- Write it down: markets, setups, risk, schedule, review.
- Rules must be objectively checkable.
- Success = following the plan.

**Practice:** Use docs/templates/TRADING-PLAN.md to write your first plan.

**Read more:** Alexander Elder, 'The New Trading for a Living' (record keeping and plan); Van K. Tharp, 'Trade Your Way to Financial Freedom'

### 5.4 Journaling and review

Professional traders keep detailed records: date, instrument, setup, entry, stop, target, exit, size, result in R, screenshot, emotion and lesson. The Trading journal in this app stores the core of this.

Weekly review questions: Did I follow my plan? Which setup made money and which lost? Were my losses caused by the market or by mistakes? What one thing will I improve next week?

Separate good decisions from good outcomes. A trade that followed the plan and lost is a good trade. A trade that broke the rules and won is a bad trade that teaches a dangerous lesson.

Over months, your journal becomes your best data source: it shows which conditions suit your strategy and where you leak money.

**Key takeaways**

- Record every trade, including emotions.
- Judge decisions, not outcomes.
- Weekly reviews compound your improvement.

**Practice:** Run a weekly review using docs/templates/WEEKLY-REVIEW.md.

**Read more:** Annie Duke, 'Thinking in Bets'; Brett Steenbarger, 'Trading Psychology 2.0'
